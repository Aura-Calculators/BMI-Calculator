    The complete guide to BMI and body metrics

    Body mass index, or BMI, is a single number that relates your weight to your height. It was devised in the 1830s by the Belgian mathematician Adolphe Quetelet and popularised as a population-level screening measure in the twentieth century. Today, health authorities including the World Health Organization use it because it is cheap, fast and repeatable. This guide explains how it is calculated, what the categories mean, where it falls short and how to combine it with other measures.

    How to calculate BMI: the formula

    In metric units, BMI equals weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. A person who weighs 68 kg and is 1.70 m tall has a BMI of 68 ÷ (1.70 × 1.70) = 23.5. If you measure height in centimetres, divide by 100 first.

    In imperial units, BMI equals 703 times weight in pounds divided by height in inches squared. Someone who weighs 150 lbs and is 67 inches tall has a BMI of 703 × 150 ÷ 4,489 = 23.5. The factor 703 simply converts pounds and inches to the metric result, so both formulas give the same answer for the same person. To convert feet and inches to inches, multiply feet by 12 and add the remaining inches.

    These bands are statistical groupings. The risk of conditions such as type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and heart disease tends to rise across the range, but there is no cliff edge at any single number. Someone at 24.8 is not meaningfully different from someone at 25.1.

    Why BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnosis

    BMI uses only two inputs. It cannot tell muscle from fat, and it says nothing about where fat is stored. That has several consequences.

    Athletes and muscular people

    Muscle is denser than fat. A strength athlete or rugby player can have a BMI in the overweight range while carrying low body fat. For these people BMI often overstates risk, and measures such as waist circumference, skinfolds or a body composition scan are more informative.

    Older adults

    With age, people tend to lose muscle and height and gain relative fat, so the same BMI can hide a very different body composition. In later life, a BMI slightly above the normal band is sometimes associated with better outcomes than a lower one, and unintentional weight loss is often a bigger concern than a slightly high number.

    Body composition and fat distribution

    Two people with the same BMI can have different health profiles. Fat stored around the abdomen, known as visceral fat, is more strongly linked to metabolic risk than fat stored on the hips and thighs. This is why waist measurements add useful context.

    WHO versus regional cutoffs

    Research has shown that, at the same BMI, people of South Asian, East Asian and some other backgrounds tend to carry more body fat and develop type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at lower BMIs than people of European descent. For that reason, WHO expert consultations and several national bodies in Asia use lower action points: roughly 23 for increased risk and 27.5 for high risk. The calculator's Asia-Pacific filter applies these cutoffs. If you are of Asian heritage, comparing both views gives a fuller picture. Other groups, such as some Pacific Islander populations, may have higher appropriate cutoffs, so talk with a clinician who knows your context.

    Children and teens

    Adult cutoffs do not apply to people aged 5 to 17. Children grow at different rates, and healthy BMI changes with age and sex. Clinicians plot a child's BMI on a growth chart and read it as a percentile for their age and sex. The WHO growth reference defines overweight and obesity in this age group by standard deviations above the median. If you are worried about a child's weight, avoid restrictive diets and speak to a paediatrician.

    Healthy weight range for your height

    Rearranging the BMI formula gives a weight range for any height. Multiply the lower cutoff (18.5) and upper cutoff (25 for WHO, 23 for Asia-Pacific) by your height in metres squared. At 1.70 m, the WHO range is about 53.5 to 72.3 kg. Treat it as a broad target zone, not a single ideal weight.

    Combining BMI with waist measurements

    The waist-to-height ratio divides waist circumference by height, using the same units for both. A ratio below 0.5 is widely used as a simple healthy marker, and values from 0.5 to 0.59 suggest more abdominal fat than ideal. Because it adjusts for height, one cut-off works across most adults, and some studies find it predicts cardiometabolic risk better than BMI alone. Measure at the midpoint between the lowest rib and the top of the hip bone, after a normal breath out, with the tape snug but not compressing the skin.

    Understanding BMR and daily calories

    Basal metabolic rate is the energy your body uses at rest. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation estimates it from weight, height, age and sex, and it is considered among the more accurate predictive equations for general populations. Multiplying BMR by an activity factor, from 1.2 for sedentary to 1.9 for very active, estimates the calories needed to maintain your weight. A modest deficit or surplus of around 10 percent is a sensible starting point, and you should adjust it based on how your weight and energy respond over several weeks.

    Sustainable weight management habits

    • Aim for gradual change. Losing around 0.25 to 0.5 kg (0.5 to 1 lb) a week is easier to sustain than crash dieting.
    • Prioritise protein and fibre. They help you feel full and protect muscle during weight loss.
    • Move daily. Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus two strength sessions.
    • Sleep 7 to 9 hours. Short sleep affects appetite hormones and makes choices harder.
    • Track trends, not days. Weight fluctuates with water and food volume. Compare weekly averages.
    • Get support. A doctor or registered dietitian can tailor advice, especially with medical conditions.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is a normal BMI range?

    For adults, the WHO defines a normal BMI as 18.5 to 24.9. Asia-Pacific guidance uses 18.5 to 22.9 as the normal band.

    Is BMI accurate for muscular people?

    Not always. BMI cannot distinguish muscle from fat, so athletes and heavily muscled people can score as overweight with low body fat. Waist measurements or body composition tests are better in that case.

    Does BMI work the same for children and teens?

    No. For ages 5 to 17, BMI is interpreted as a percentile for age and sex using growth charts. Adult categories do not apply, which is why this calculator shows a youth note.

    How do I convert between metric and imperial units?

    One kilogram is 2.2046 pounds, and one inch is 2.54 centimetres. The calculator converts your entered values automatically when you switch units.

    Is BMI reliable during pregnancy?

    No. BMI is not designed for pregnancy. Pre-pregnancy BMI is used by clinicians to guide weight-gain recommendations, so ask your midwife or doctor.

    Should older adults use the same BMI ranges?

    Many guidelines accept a slightly higher BMI for older adults, since muscle loss and frailty are important risks. Discuss your results with your doctor.

    What is the waist-to-height ratio?

    It is your waist circumference divided by your height. A value under 0.5 is generally seen as healthier, and higher values suggest more abdominal fat.

    How accurate are the calorie and body fat estimates?

    They are population-based estimates and can be off by a meaningful margin for an individual. Use them as a starting point and adjust to real-world results.

    Can BMI diagnose a health condition?

    No. BMI is a screening tool. A diagnosis needs a clinician who considers your history, blood tests, blood pressure, waist size and more.
